The FERS Supplement Explained: Who Gets It and How Much

Published: 2026-08-07 • Reviewed by: Shawn McCoy • Last reviewed: 2026-08-07

The FERS Supplement Explained: Who Gets It and How Much

Retiring before 62 can create a confusing income gap: your FERS annuity may begin, but Social Security may still be years away. That is where the Special Retirement Supplement fits into the retirement picture. It is separate from Social Security itself, and it follows specific eligibility, calculation, timing, and earnings rules.

The FERS supplement is a temporary monthly payment for certain FERS employees who retire before age 62, designed to help bridge the gap until Social Security eligibility. The amount is based on an estimate of your Social Security benefit and your years of FERS service, not simply on your federal salary. An earnings test may reduce or eliminate the payment before age 62.

The first question is whether your retirement meets the service and age requirements. Those rules differ for regular FERS employees and workers covered by special retirement provisions. So eligibility should be examined before relying on the supplement in a retirement income plan.

Who Qualifies for the FERS Supplement?

The FERS annuity supplement, also called the Special Retirement Supplement, is not automatic for every FERS retiree. It is designed to help bridge the income gap for certain employees who retire before age 62, when Social Security eligibility begins. The key question is whether your retirement meets an immediate, unreduced eligibility rule rather than a deferred or postponed retirement path.

Minimum Retirement Age plus 30 years of service

The standard path is retiring at your Minimum Retirement Age (MRA) with at least 30 years of creditable federal service. Your MRA depends on your birth year, so it is not the same for every employee. Meeting the MRA and service requirement is only the starting point. Other rules, including the earnings test after MRA and before age 62, can later reduce or eliminate the payment.

Be careful when comparing an immediate retirement with postponed retirement and FERS benefits. Leaving federal service and claiming a deferred annuity generally does not create the same supplement entitlement as an immediate retirement under FERS rules.

Age 60 or 61 plus 20 years of service

You may also qualify by retiring at age 60 or 61 with at least 20 years of service. This route does not require waiting until age 62, and it is separate from the MRA-plus-30-years route. Your individual service record still matters, including whether the service is creditable and whether your retirement is immediate under the applicable FERS rules.

Review the details that affect your broader FERS retirement eligibility factors before treating a service total as final. Sick leave credit, for example, may affect the annuity calculation, but eligibility rules and creditable-service rules should be reviewed carefully.

Special coverage employees

Some employees with special retirement coverage can qualify earlier than the standard MRA. Covered law enforcement officers and firefighters may be eligible as early as age 50 with 20 years of covered service. Or with 25 years of covered service at any age allowed under the special provision. Air traffic controllers may have different mandatory retirement rules. Mandatory retirement is generally age 57 for most special coverage employees and age 56 for air traffic controllers, subject to the rules governing the position.

These categories are not interchangeable with ordinary FERS service. The work must meet the applicable special coverage definition, and the required years must be covered service. Because position coverage and retirement timing can change the result, confirm the record with authoritative OPM materials and your agency retirement office before making an irreversible decision. The OPM CSRS and FERS Handbook describes the annuity supplement as payable to certain FERS retirees who retire before age 62.

How the FERS Supplement Is Calculated

The estimate is designed to approximate the Social Security benefit you earned during your federal career, then adjust it for the number of years you worked under FERS. It is not simply a percentage of your high-3 salary. OPM uses an earnings history that combines your actual pay with deemed pay based on your FERS service. Then applies a process modeled on Social Security's Average Indexed Monthly Earnings (AIME) and Primary Insurance Amount (PIA) formulas. The OPM CSRS/FERS Handbook describes the calculation in detail.

A practical three-step estimate

You can create a planning estimate before OPM issues its final calculation. Use your latest Social Security statement and follow these steps:

  1. Get your Social Security estimate at age 62. Sign in to your personal account at ssa.gov and review your statement. Use the projected monthly retirement benefit shown for age 62, not the amount for full retirement age or age 70.
  2. Multiply that estimate by your total years of FERS service. Round your years of FERS service up to the next whole year for this planning estimate. For example, 27 years and several months would be treated as 28 years in the estimate.
  3. Divide the result by 40. The 40 represents a full Social Security career. The resulting amount is an approximate monthly FERS supplement before any applicable earnings-test reduction.

Why the calculation uses a 40-year career model

OPM effectively asks what Social Security benefit you might have earned if your FERS-covered service had been part of a complete 40-year career. It constructs the relevant earnings history from pay you actually received and deemed pay assigned to your FERS service. That history is then used with the AIME and PIA concepts used in Social Security calculations. Your actual supplement can therefore differ from a quick worksheet estimate, especially when your earnings record, service history, or retirement circumstances require a more detailed review.

For a refresher on the service and salary figures that affect your federal retirement calculation, review this guide to basic FERS pension math. Treat any result from the three steps as a planning estimate, not an official OPM determination.

When the FERS Supplement Stops at Age 62

The FERS supplement is designed to bridge the gap between an eligible FERS retirement and age 62, when Social Security first becomes available. That bridge has a firm end date. According to the Office of Personnel Management, the payment stops at the end of the month before you turn 62.

For example, if your 62nd birthday is in October, your final FERS supplement payment is for September. It does not continue through your birthday month, and it does not remain in place until you claim Social Security.

The supplement ending does not force a Social Security filing

Losing the supplement at 62 and claiming Social Security at 62 are separate decisions. OPM states that you are not required to apply for Social Security at age 62 just because the supplement ends. You can decide when to apply based on your broader retirement income plan, eligibility details, and personal circumstances. If you delay your application, however, you will need to plan for the income gap after the supplement ends.

That distinction matters when you compare your monthly income before and after age 62. The supplement is temporary. It is not an early version of your Social Security benefit that remains available until you choose to start Social Security.

The supplement does not receive regular FERS pension COLAs

The FERS supplement also differs from the base FERS pension because it does not receive the same cost-of-living adjustments. In practical terms, the supplement is not designed to rise with inflation during the years it is paid. The base annuity and the temporary supplement should therefore be tracked as separate income sources when you estimate future purchasing power. Review FERS pension stability for more context on how inflation can affect retirement income.

Before retiring, map the month the supplement ends, your expected base annuity, and your Social Security filing decision separately. This simple timeline can make the transition at 62 easier to understand.

How the FERS Supplement Earnings Test Works

The earnings test is one of the most misunderstood parts of the FERS Supplement. It does not apply simply because you retire before your Minimum Retirement Age (MRA). Instead, after you reach your MRA and before the supplement ends at age 62. The Office of Personnel Management (OPM) applies the Social Security annual earnings limit to your work income. Depending on how much you earn, OPM can reduce or eliminate the monthly supplement. The test is described in this overview of the FERS Supplement.

What counts as earnings?

For this test, the relevant income is generally earned income from work. That includes wages and net income from self-employment. It does not include your FERS pension, other pension payments, TSP withdrawals, or investment income. In other words, the test focuses on compensation for working, not every dollar that may come into your household.

How the reduction is calculated

The annual exempt amount changes as the Social Security earnings limit changes. For 2025, the threshold is $23,400. If your covered earnings stay at or below that amount, the earnings test does not reduce the supplement. If you earn more, the reduction is $1 for every $2 above the limit. This is an annual calculation, so compare your expected wages and self-employment income for the calendar year with the applicable limit. Rather than comparing only one month of pay.

For example, $4,000 above the annual limit would produce a $2,000 reduction in the supplement for the applicable period. If the reduction is larger than the supplement payable, the payment can be eliminated. The calculation is not a penalty on your pension. It is a limit on the earnings-tested supplement, applied by OPM under the Social Security earnings-limit framework. See the 2025 threshold discussion for the published $23,400 figure.

The special limit in the year you reach full retirement age

A higher final-year limit can apply, but it is easy to misuse this rule. The $62,160 limit cited for 2025 applies only during the calendar year in which you reach Social Security full retirement age. It is not a general limit for every year before age 62, and it does not mean the standard $23,400 limit can be ignored in earlier years. Because limits and full retirement age rules can change, confirm the amount for your year with current Social Security and OPM information before estimating your payment.

The practical takeaway is to track work income separately from pension, TSP, and investment income, then review the applicable annual limit for each calendar year before age 62. The earnings test affects the supplement, not your underlying FERS retirement eligibility or base annuity.

FERS Supplement Example: A Worked Illustration

A worked example can make the formula easier to follow. The figures below are illustrative estimates only. They are not a quote, promise, or guarantee of what any retiree will receive. OPM calculates the actual amount using your earnings record and service information.

Step 1: Start with the Social Security estimate

Assume a federal employee reviews a Social Security statement showing an estimated benefit of $1,800 per month at age 62. The employee retires with 30 years of creditable FERS service. The estimate uses the general 40-year career approach described in the OPM CSRS and FERS Handbook. Chapter 51, which explains the relationship to Average Indexed Monthly Earnings and the Primary Insurance Amount.

For this illustration:

$1,800 x 30 years of FERS service / 40 years = $1,350 per month

Under these assumptions, the estimated FERS supplement would be $1,350 per month before any earnings-test reduction. The calculation is designed to approximate the Social Security benefit earned during the FERS career, rather than treating the employee's entire Social Security estimate as the supplement.

Step 2: Apply the earnings test

Now assume the retiree has $12,000 in earnings above the 2025 annual exempt amount of $23,400. The earnings test reduces the supplement by $1 for every $2 earned over the limit. That produces a $6,000 reduction for the year:

$12,000 / 2 = $6,000 annual reduction

Dividing that annual reduction across 12 months equals $500 per month. If applied evenly for illustration, the monthly estimate changes as follows:

$1,350 - $500 = $850 per month

The actual timing and amount of any adjustment may differ from this simplified example. The earnings test applies during the relevant period after Minimum Retirement Age and before the supplement ends. OPM reviews the applicable earnings information, and the annual limit can change.

What this example does not show

Your result may differ because OPM's computation uses your specific earnings history, including actual and deemed pay associated with FERS service. Service credit, retirement timing, covered earnings, and reported income all matter. Treat a quick formula as a planning starting point, not a final benefits determination.

FERS Supplement vs. Waiting to 62: A Quick Comparison

How the two retirement timing options differ
Scenario Retire at MRA with supplement Wait to age 62
Pension multiplier Generally 1.0% per year of service. 1.1% per year with at least 20 years of service.
Supplement Paid until the end of the month before age 62, if eligible. None, because the supplement is designed for eligible retirees under 62.
Earnings test Applies after MRA and before age 62. None on a FERS supplement.
COLA on supplement No COLA on the supplement. Not applicable.

Retiring at MRA can provide a monthly bridge between your FERS retirement and age 62. However, eligible retirees who earn income before 62 may see the supplement reduced or eliminated under the earnings test. The limit changes by year. For example, the cited 2025 threshold was $23,400, and the Social Security earnings rules determine the applicable limit. See the eligibility and earnings-test overview for context.

Waiting until 62 can remove the bridge payment, but the higher 1.1% multiplier is permanent when the 20-year service requirement is met. The supplement also ends before 62 and does not receive the same cost-of-living adjustments as the base FERS pension. So compare both the temporary income and the lasting annuity difference before choosing a retirement date. These are general rules, not an individualized retirement recommendation.

Is the FERS Supplement Going Away in 2028?

Short answer: no current law eliminates the FERS Supplement in 2028. The concern came from a proposal connected to H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. The House-passed version included a provision that would have ended the supplement for people who were not yet entitled to it before January 1, 2028.

That provision did not survive the legislative process. Before the Senate passed its version, the Senate parliamentarian ruled that the supplement language violated the Byrd Rule. Which limits what provisions can be included in certain budget reconciliation bills. The language was removed. The enacted law, Public Law 119-21, signed July 4, 2025, contains no changes to the FERS annuity supplement. The official bill record is available through Congress.gov.

What the current rules still say

Under the current rules, the FERS annuity supplement remains a bridge payment for certain FERS retirees who leave federal service before age 62. It generally ends at the end of the month before the retiree reaches age 62. OPM also makes clear that the supplement ending does not require you to claim Social Security at 62. The decision about when to apply for Social Security remains yours. You can review the governing details in OPM's CSRS and FERS Handbook.

Could Congress revisit it later?

Long-term political risk is different from an enacted change. Similar proposals affecting the supplement have appeared in federal budget documents since 2013, so future legislation is possible. However, as of August 2026, no active legislation targets the FERS Supplement. A proposal, budget document, or news report should not be treated as a change to your benefits until Congress passes it and it becomes law.

If you are planning retirement, use the rules in effect today for your initial estimate, then monitor official OPM and congressional sources for actual changes. This is general education, not an individualized benefits determination.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the FERS supplement still available?

Yes. Eligible FERS employees who retire before age 62 may receive this monthly bridge benefit between retirement and Social Security eligibility. It is not automatic for every FERS retirement, so your age, service history, retirement type, and coverage category matter. Review more federal benefits retirement questions as you compare your options.

Who qualifies for this benefit?

Standard eligibility generally includes retiring at your Minimum Retirement Age with at least 30 years of service. Or retiring at age 60 or 61 with at least 20 years. Special coverage employees, including some law enforcement officers and firefighters, may qualify earlier with covered service. Confirm the exact rule for your position with OPM before making a retirement decision. OPM eligibility rules are summarized here.

How is the FERS supplement calculated?

OPM estimates the Social Security benefit you earned through FERS service, then applies the proportion represented by your FERS years compared with a full 40-year career. The calculation uses an earnings history that can include actual pay and deemed pay. Your estimate can therefore differ from a Social Security statement based only on reported wages. See OPM's FERS handbook calculation guidance.

What happens when the payment ends at age 62?

The supplement stops at the end of the month before you turn 62. You do not have to claim Social Security at 62, however. The decision about when to apply for Social Security remains yours, so plan for the income change if you delay your claim. OPM confirms both rules.

How does the earnings test affect the supplement?

After you reach your Minimum Retirement Age and before the supplement ends, earnings from work can reduce or eliminate the payment. The threshold changes annually with Social Security earnings limits; for 2025, the cited threshold was $23,400. OPM applies the test to covered earnings, so review current limits and reportable income before returning to work. See the earnings-test overview.
