TSP Annual Contribution Limits for Federal Employees: What You Can Contribute in 2025 and 2026

Published: October 9, 2026 • Reviewed by: Shawn McCoy • Last reviewed: 2026-10-09

Request a guide to tsp annual contribution limits for federal employees what you can contribute in 2025 and 2026, with catch-up and payroll tips.

By Dr. FedEd Shawn McCoy. TSP annual contribution limits for federal employees can change from year to year, and the amount you may contribute can also depend on your age and pay schedule. Here is a plain-English look at the 2025 and 2026 limits, what counts toward them, and a few practical ways to keep contributions on track.

What Is the TSP Annual Contribution Limit?

The annual elective deferral limit is the most you can contribute from your own pay to your TSP in a calendar year. It applies to your combined traditional and Roth TSP contributions. Choosing both tax treatments does not give you two separate employee limits.

For 2025, the standard employee contribution limit was $23,500. For 2026, it is $24,500. The Internal Revenue Service announced the 2026 limit, and the Thrift Savings Plan publishes the applicable contribution limits and catch-up details. You can check the 2026 IRS limit announcement and the TSP contribution limits page for official updates.

These are calendar-year limits. They are not a required contribution amount, a promise about investment performance, or a recommendation about how much any one person should save. A contribution election that works well for one employee may not fit another person’s budget, timeline, or other retirement resources.

What Are the 2025 and 2026 TSP Limits?

The table compares the basic employee limit and the age-based catch-up limits. Catch-up contributions are available to eligible participants; they are not an extra allowance for every participant. Confirm your eligibility and the current-year details on the official TSP site before changing your election.

Contribution limit20252026Who it may apply to
Standard employee limit$23,500$24,500Employee contributions, traditional and Roth combined
Age-50-and-older catch-up limit$7,500$8,000Eligible participants age 50 or older by year-end
Higher catch-up limit for ages 60–63Up to $11,250Up to $11,250Eligible participants who reach age 60, 61, 62, or 63 during the year, subject to the applicable rules

The basic limits are confirmed in the IRS’s 2026 announcement. The TSP’s official 2025 contribution limits bulletin describes that year’s limits, while its current contribution limits guidance explains current catch-up rules. Rules and limits can change, so use the official information for the year in which the contributions will be made.

The higher age-60-through-63 catch-up is a special limit for eligible participants in that age range. It is not added on top of the regular age-50 catch-up limit as a second catch-up. For a quick orientation, the annual maximum for an eligible participant is generally the standard employee limit plus the applicable catch-up limit. Check the TSP guidance to confirm which catch-up amount applies to your situation and year.

Reading the table carefully helps avoid a common mix-up: the catch-up figure is an additional amount that may be available to an eligible participant, not the total annual limit. For example, if the standard limit is $24,500 and the applicable catch-up limit is $8,000, the combined potential employee amount is $32,500, subject to eligibility and plan rules. An eligible participant in the age-60-through-63 range should use that year’s higher catch-up figure instead of stacking it on top of the usual age-50 catch-up.

Think of the annual limit as a cap on employee elective deferrals, not as a required deposit or a balance target. The limit also does not say whether you should make traditional contributions, Roth contributions, or a combination. Those are separate choices with tax considerations, and this guide does not determine which choice is suitable for you.

Do Traditional and Roth TSP Contributions Have Separate Limits?

No. Your traditional and Roth employee contributions share the same annual elective deferral limit. For example, if you direct some pay to traditional TSP and some to Roth TSP, the combined amount is what counts toward the annual employee limit.

That distinction matters when you adjust your payroll election. If you contribute to both types, track the total rather than treating each contribution source as having its own full limit. Your tax treatment for contributions and later withdrawals can differ, but the annual employee contribution cap is shared.

For more on the difference between the two tax treatments, see the Federal Benefits Exchange guide to Roth versus traditional TSP contributions. That discussion is separate from deciding how much to contribute; this article does not recommend a tax choice for you.

Do Agency or Service Contributions Count Toward Your Employee Limit?

Agency or service contributions are separate from the elective deferrals you make from your own pay. They do not use up the standard employee elective deferral limit shown above. They do, however, belong to the broader set of contributions credited to your TSP account, which has its own annual limits under applicable rules.

This is one reason to distinguish “my contribution limit” from every dollar that may enter the account. Your own payroll contributions, agency or service contributions, and the plan’s overall annual additions rules are related, but they are not identical. If you need to understand how matching contributions work, review the official TSP explanation of contributions and the plan information provided by your agency or service.

Federal employees covered by FERS should also understand how their own contribution election interacts with the agency contribution structure. The guide to TSP contributions and the agency match covers that separate question. It is useful to keep the match discussion distinct from the annual employee limit: the match does not create a second employee deferral allowance.

As a practical matter, separate the numbers when you review your account. Your earnings statement may show the amount withheld from your pay, while your account activity can also reflect employer contributions. When checking whether you are near your employee limit, focus on the employee deferrals and make sure traditional and Roth amounts are both included. For questions about how an item is labeled or posted, ask the payroll or benefits office rather than estimating from the total account balance.

How Do Catch-Up Contributions Work?

Catch-up contributions let eligible participants contribute above the standard employee limit when they meet the age requirements. The familiar catch-up rule applies to eligible participants age 50 or older by the end of the calendar year. A higher catch-up limit may apply for eligible participants who are ages 60 through 63 during that year.

The limit is annual, so your contribution pace matters. A participant who begins catch-up contributions late in the year may need a larger per-pay-period deduction to reach a chosen annual amount. Whether payroll can make the required adjustment in time depends on the remaining pay dates and the agency or service’s processing deadlines.

Current rules may require certain higher-paid participants to make catch-up contributions as Roth contributions. This is a rule to verify against current TSP guidance, not a reason to assume the same treatment applies to every participant. Review the details on the TSP’s contribution limits page and ask your benefits office about payroll implementation if you are unsure.

For example, an employee who turns 50 during a calendar year should not assume catch-up eligibility begins only on their birthday; the relevant rule refers to age during the year. Still, age eligibility is only one part of the calculation. Confirm the annual limit, applicable catch-up category, and any current requirements before setting a payroll amount. If you are in the age-60-through-63 range, verify that the higher catch-up amount applies in the specific year you are planning for.

Catch-up room can also be affected by timing. Someone who changes an election early in the year has more pay dates in which to spread the amount. Someone who makes the decision late in the year may face a much larger per-pay-period figure, and payroll may not process a late change before the final deductions. A simple estimate is useful for planning, but verify the election’s effective date and check the actual deductions that follow.

How Can You Pace Contributions Across the Year?

A simple estimate can help you compare your current payroll election with the annual limit. First, choose a contribution amount to evaluate. Then subtract what you have already contributed this calendar year and divide the remainder by the number of pay dates left. The result is a rough per-pay-period amount, not a recommendation.

  1. Check year-to-date contributions. Use your latest earnings statement or TSP account information. Make sure you are looking at the correct calendar year and include both traditional and Roth employee contributions.
  2. Choose the limit that applies. Check the standard limit or applicable catch-up limit for the year, based on your age and eligibility.
  3. Count remaining pay periods. Use the pay calendar and leave room for payroll processing deadlines or a delayed election change.
  4. Estimate the remaining amount. Subtract year-to-date employee contributions from the amount you are evaluating, then divide by the remaining pay dates.
  5. Recheck after a change. Review subsequent earnings statements to make sure the intended election took effect and the year-to-date total is moving as expected.

For example, suppose an employee has contributed $12,000 toward the regular 2026 employee limit and has 12 pay dates remaining. The remaining difference between $24,500 and $12,000 is $12,500. Dividing that amount by 12 gives roughly $1,042 per pay date. That is arithmetic for illustration only; it does not account for take-home pay, other payroll deductions, or an individual’s circumstances.

Here is a second way to use the same arithmetic. Suppose a participant is evaluating a total annual employee contribution of $32,500 because the standard 2026 limit and an $8,000 age-50 catch-up appear applicable. If the participant has already contributed $18,000 and has 10 pay dates left, the remaining amount to reach that illustrative total is $14,500, or $1,450 per remaining pay date. The figure is not a recommended deduction. The participant must first verify eligibility and then consider whether the amount fits the paycheck and payroll rules.

The calculation changes if the actual amount already contributed is different from the figure you expected. For instance, if a midyear review shows $600 more in employee contributions than your own worksheet, use the verified year-to-date number in the subtraction. Do not continue using an old estimate because it makes the planned deduction look easier. Reconcile any difference between payroll and account information before making a large adjustment.

Some employees think in percentages rather than fixed dollars. If your election is a percentage of pay, translate that percentage into an approximate dollar amount for each remaining pay period, then compare the projected total with the limit. Pay can vary, and deductions may be calculated under payroll rules, so a percentage-based projection is only an estimate. Check actual contributions over the next few statements rather than assuming each paycheck will match your forecast.

Some payroll systems stop or change deductions when a participant reaches a limit, while timing and agency procedures can affect how contributions are processed. Do not rely on a calculation alone as confirmation that a payroll election has been accepted. Follow up through your payroll or benefits channel and check the account record.

What If You Reach the Limit Before the End of the Year?

Reaching the employee elective deferral limit does not mean you should guess what will happen to later deductions. Review the TSP and payroll instructions for your employment category. In particular, understand how the timing of contributions may affect any agency or service contributions tied to pay-period contributions. Do not assume that a year-end limit adjustment will work the same way for every payroll system.

If your contributions stop earlier than expected, check your year-to-date total, the effective date of the last election change, and the number of pay periods remaining. If you see a difference between the election you requested and what appears on your earnings statement, contact the appropriate payroll or benefits office. The TSP’s official contribution information is a good reference, but your payroll channel can explain processing for your agency or service.

It can help to keep a small record of each change: the date you submitted it, the effective pay period shown by the system, the requested contribution type, and the deduction on the first paycheck after the change. This is not a substitute for official records. It gives you a straightforward way to describe the issue if the election does not appear as expected. Keep copies of relevant earnings statements and note the date you contacted payroll, especially near year-end.

If you change agencies, enter or leave federal service, or have an unusual payroll interruption, do not assume a simple full-year estimate applies. Your contributions may have been made over a different number of pay periods than the calendar year suggests. Check the current plan record, ask the relevant benefits office how the payroll transition is handled, and confirm which amounts count toward the calendar-year employee limit.

For a practical account overview, the Exchange’s TSP guide for federal employees and guide to reading a federal leave and earnings statement may help you locate relevant plan and payroll details. Your earnings statement is not a substitute for official plan records, so compare information when something does not line up.

What Should You Check Before Changing Your Election?

Before increasing, lowering, or dividing contributions, take a moment to review the moving parts. These checks can help you ask clearer questions without turning a limit calculation into a full retirement decision.

  • Calendar year: Make sure you are applying the limit for the year in which the contributions are made.
  • Combined employee contributions: Add traditional and Roth payroll contributions together.
  • Age and eligibility: Confirm whether a standard or higher catch-up rule applies for the year.
  • Year-to-date amount: Compare your records with current payroll and TSP information.
  • Pay schedule: Count remaining pay dates and note election-change deadlines.
  • Other priorities: Consider take-home pay and your broader benefits picture. A contribution limit is a ceiling, not a target that everyone must reach.

You may find it useful to turn those checks into a short worksheet before you contact payroll. Write down the tax year, the standard or catch-up limit you are using, year-to-date traditional deductions, year-to-date Roth deductions, and the number of pay dates remaining. Then write down the amount you are trying to evaluate and the arithmetic that produced your per-pay-period estimate. If the numbers do not reconcile, pause the estimate and confirm the source figures.

Keep separate questions separate. One question is whether an amount is within the annual limit. Another is whether an election can be processed before a deadline. A third is whether a contribution amount fits your household budget or broader retirement plan. Official plan and payroll channels can help with the first two; an annual cap by itself cannot answer the third.

You can also review the Federal Benefits Exchange FAQ or its federal benefits resource guide for further educational starting points. For questions about your official account, payroll deductions, or eligibility, use the appropriate TSP and agency channels.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the 2026 TSP contribution limit for federal employees?

The standard employee elective deferral limit for 2026 is $24,500. Eligible participants may also qualify for an age-based catch-up limit. Check official TSP guidance for the rules that apply to your age and circumstances.

Do Roth and traditional TSP contributions have separate annual limits?

No. Your traditional and Roth employee contributions share one annual elective deferral limit. Add the two together when tracking your employee contributions for the year.

Can participants age 50 or older contribute more than the standard limit?

Eligible participants age 50 or older may be able to make catch-up contributions. A higher catch-up limit may apply to eligible participants who are ages 60 through 63 during the year. The exact limit depends on the contribution year and applicable rules.

Do agency or service contributions count toward my employee contribution limit?

Agency or service contributions do not use up your employee elective deferral limit. They are subject to separate plan rules, including broader limits on total contributions, so check official TSP information for details.

Where can I verify the limit for a particular year?

Use the official TSP contribution limits page and IRS announcements for the year in question. Your agency or service payroll office can help clarify how election changes and contributions are processed. A careful check of the rules and your year-to-date amounts can make the next step easier.

Take the limit as a useful boundary, not a personal target: review the current rules, check your records, and make contribution decisions in the context of your full benefits picture.

The full election costs $450 per month out of your paycheck — $5,400 per year. That's real money. It is easy to look at that number and decide it isn't worth it, especially when you are healthy and expect to live a long time.

But consider what your spouse faces without it:

  • No monthly income from your pension
  • No continuation of federal health insurance (more on that below)
  • Dependent on their own Social Security, any personal savings, and Federal Employees' Group Life Insurance (FEGLI) life insurance if you carried it — though you should watch out for steep FEGLI Option B cost increases in retirement

The survivor annuity isn't a luxury. For many spouses, it's the floor of their financial security.

The Break-Even Question

Some advisors frame the survivor annuity as a financial bet: if you live long enough, you "win" by keeping the full annuity. If you die early, your spouse "wins" by collecting the survivor benefit.

That framing misses the point. The survivor annuity isn't just about income. It's about risk — specifically, the catastrophic risk of a widow or widower outliving their resources. A 62-year-old spouse who suddenly loses the household pension income and health coverage faces a financial emergency, not a minor adjustment.

The Hidden Gotcha: FEHB Coverage Requires a Survivor Annuity

This is the fact that shocks most federal employees — and it should be the headline of every pre-retirement seminar.

If you elect no survivor annuity, your spouse cannot continue FEHB health insurance coverage after your death. This is the essence of the spousal FEHB healthcare trap, which can permanently strip your surviving spouse of their health insurance.

Let that sink in. Federal Employees Health Benefits — the same coverage your family has depended on throughout your career — is available to a surviving spouse only if a survivor annuity is payable. OPM's rule is explicit: for a surviving spouse to continue FEHB enrollment, at least one family member must be entitled to receive a FERS or Civil Service Retirement System (CSRS) survivor annuity (under 5 C.F.R. § 890.306).

USPS employees and annuitants: Starting in 2025, the Postal Service Health Benefits Program (PSHB) replaced FEHB for postal workers. The survivor continuity principle is the same — a surviving spouse can generally continue PSHB coverage only as an eligible survivor annuitant. If no survivor annuity is payable, health coverage access ends. The planning lesson is identical: do not evaluate the survivor election in isolation from health coverage.

If no survivor annuity is payable, OPM offers the surviving spouse exactly one thing: a one-time opportunity to convert to private health coverage through the insurance carrier. There is no grace period, no waiver, and no appeal process if the election was never made.

What Losing FEHB Means in Practice

FEHB is among the most complete group health insurance plans available to non-military Americans. Federal retirees who carry FEHB into retirement keep it for life — with the federal government still contributing to the premium. It covers a wide range of plans, networks, and prescription benefits.

A surviving spouse who loses FEHB access must find coverage elsewhere. Options typically include:

  • COBRA continuation — available but expensive, and limited to 36 months
  • ACA marketplace plans — premium costs depend on income and age, and can run $600–$1,200+ per month for a person in their early 60s
  • Medicare — not available until age 65; a 60-year-old widow has a five-year gap to cover. For details on how coverage coordinates once they turn 65, see our guide on Medicare Part B enrollment rules.

For a spouse who is years away from Medicare eligibility, losing FEHB can mean thousands of additional dollars per year in healthcare costs — layered on top of the already-devastating loss of pension income.

This is not a hypothetical problem. Financial planners who work with federal employees see it repeatedly: a surviving spouse, already grieving, discovers that the health insurance they assumed was lifetime coverage has vanished because a single election was skipped twenty years ago.

The Spouse Must Consent to Waive This Protection

There is a safeguard built into the system. If you retire married and want to elect a partial survivor benefit or no survivor benefit at all, your spouse must sign Form SF-3107-2 — the Spouse's Consent to Survivor Election — in the presence of a notary (see OPM CSRS/FERS Handbook, Chapter 52 for formal requirements).

This requirement exists precisely because Congress recognized the stakes. Your spouse deserves to know what they're agreeing to before you hand over their financial security.

If OPM receives your retirement application without the required notarized consent form and you elected less than the full survivor benefit, OPM will default to the maximum election. This permanently reduces your annuity — even if it wasn't your intention — because protecting the uninformed spouse takes priority.

The consent requirement also matters in the other direction: if you and your spouse together decide to waive the benefit, that decision should be made with full knowledge of all its consequences, including the FEHB impact. That conversation is exactly the kind of planning an annual Pay Stub Review (PSR) helps with.

Can Private Life Insurance Fill the Gap?

Some financial strategies — sometimes called "pension maximization" — propose that a federal employee elect no survivor benefit, keep the full annuity, and use the savings to purchase a private life insurance policy. The idea is that the policy pays out a lump sum when the retiree dies, which the spouse invests to replace the lost income.

This approach can work in specific circumstances, but it carries real risks that federal employees should weigh carefully:

  • The FEHB problem remains unsolved. Life insurance proceeds can replace income, but they cannot replace FEHB enrollment eligibility. A surviving spouse still loses federal health coverage.
  • Insurability isn't guaranteed. If health issues arise before you execute this strategy, you may not qualify for a life insurance policy at an affordable premium — or at all.
  • Discipline is required. A lump-sum death benefit only replaces an annuity if the surviving spouse manages and invests it carefully over decades.
  • You could outlive the math. If you live significantly longer than projected, the premium costs may exceed what you would have paid for the survivor reduction anyway.

Where private life insurance genuinely shines is as a supplement to the survivor annuity — not a replacement. Whole life policies, in particular, can provide a tax-advantaged death benefit, build cash value, and fill coverage gaps that the federal system doesn't address. American Amicable offers whole life products well-suited to this supplemental role for federal employees, without requiring you to gamble your spouse's health coverage on an annuity waiver.

The right strategy depends on your specific numbers, your spouse's age and health, your total retirement income picture, and your risk tolerance. That's not a decision to make based on a general rule of thumb.

If You Die Before You Retire

The survivor benefit discussion above assumes you live to retire. The rules look different if a FERS employee dies while still actively employed.

If certain service and marriage requirements are met, a surviving spouse may qualify for a Basic Employee Death Benefit — a lump-sum payment (detailed in OPM CSRS/FERS Handbook, Chapter 70 and on OPM's FERS Survivors page). If the deceased had enough creditable service, the spouse may also qualify for a monthly survivor benefit based on that service.

This matters for federal employees who are not near retirement. Your spouse's protection depends heavily on when death occurs:

  • While you are still employed (different benefit formulas apply)
  • After you separate but before retirement (reduced protections)
  • After you retire (survivor annuity election governs)
  • After divorce or remarriage (court orders may complicate things)

This is one of the most overlooked reasons to review survivor benefits annually — not just when retirement paperwork lands on your desk.

Former Spouses and Court Orders

Survivor benefits can become significantly more complicated after divorce. A former spouse may be entitled to survivor benefits if a qualifying court order is in place. This can affect what a current spouse receives at retirement and what you are allowed to elect.

Federal employees who have divorced, remarried, or have old court orders should not assume everything updates automatically. Federal benefits are administrative — paperwork governs, not assumptions. A QDRO (Qualified Domestic Relations Order) or similar federal-specific court order can legally obligate a survivor annuity to a former spouse, potentially limiting what you can elect for a current spouse.

If any of these situations apply, review your obligations with a qualified federal benefits specialist well before your retirement application is submitted.

The Seven Most Common Survivor Benefit Mistakes

  • Mistake 1: Looking only at the monthly reduction. The 10% or 5% reduction is real — but the right question is what your spouse would lose without the benefit, not just what you'd save by skipping it.
  • Mistake 2: Ignoring health coverage. The FEHB (or PSHB for USPS) continuity issue catches people completely off guard at the worst possible moment.
  • Mistake 3: Assuming Social Security will fill the gap. Social Security survivor benefits may help — a surviving spouse may receive the higher of the two Social Security benefits — but they don't replace the full FERS annuity. Don't assume the federal pension loss is automatically covered.
  • Mistake 4: Forgetting TSP withdrawal pressure. If the surviving spouse loses pension income, they may need to draw down the TSP much faster than planned. Larger withdrawals accelerate tax exposure and shorten the life of the account.
  • Mistake 5: Assuming life insurance replaces every function of the survivor annuity. Life insurance proceeds can replace income, but they cannot restore FEHB/PSHB enrollment eligibility. The comparison has to include health coverage, not just dollars.
  • Mistake 6: Not updating beneficiary forms. The survivor annuity election, the TSP-3 form, the FEGLI SF-2823 designation, and unpaid compensation forms are all separate. An old or missing beneficiary designation can send death benefits to the wrong person regardless of your survivor election. Make sure you protect your estate by updating your TSP-3 beneficiary form and other beneficiary designations.
  • Mistake 7: Waiting until retirement paperwork arrives. The worst time to discover a problem is during the final weeks before your retirement date. Survivor benefit planning should happen years before retirement and be reviewed annually.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can I change my survivor benefit election after I retire?

Generally, no. The survivor annuity election made at retirement is permanent. There is a limited exception if you and your spouse divorce after retirement and later remarry, which may trigger a new election opportunity. But in most cases, the election you make on your retirement application is the one your spouse lives with permanently.

Q: What if my spouse has their own federal job or federal retirement? Do we still need to elect a survivor annuity?

If your spouse has their own FERS or CSRS entitlement, they may be able to keep FEHB through their own employment or retirement — meaning FEHB continuity may not depend on your election. However, they would still lose the income from your survivor annuity. This is a situation where personalized planning matters, because the right answer differs by couple.

Q: What happens to the survivor annuity if my spouse dies before me?

If your spouse predeceases you and you elected a survivor benefit, your full annuity is generally restored. You do not continue paying the reduced amount after the protected spouse is gone — you recoup that reduction going forward.

Q: What does "unreduced annuity" mean in the context of survivor benefit calculations?

OPM calculates survivor benefits based on your unreduced annuity — meaning your pension before any voluntary reductions (like a survivor annuity reduction or a Social Security offset). This matters because the 50% or 25% your spouse receives is calculated from that higher baseline number, not from whatever reduced amount you were actually collecting.

Q: Is there any way to get FEHB coverage for a surviving spouse if no survivor annuity was elected?

No, with one exception: if the surviving spouse has their own entitlement to FEHB through their own federal employment or retirement, they can continue that coverage independently. Otherwise, OPM offers only a one-time conversion to private coverage, with no ongoing federal premium contribution and no guarantee of equivalent benefits.

The Bottom Line: This Is Not the Place to Save Money

Federal retirement planning involves dozens of decisions. Many of them are reversible. The survivor annuity election is not.

The 10% reduction for a full survivor benefit is real, and it shows up every month in your paycheck for the rest of your life. It's understandable that retirees look at that number and hesitate. But the alternative — a spouse left without income, without federal health insurance, and without options — is a far worse outcome.
